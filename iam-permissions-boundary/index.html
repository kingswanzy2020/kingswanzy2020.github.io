Case study — AWS / Security

An IAM permissions boundary as a ceiling on a role

I attached a customer-managed policy to an IAM role as its permissions boundary and used the policy simulator to evaluate the same widened identity policy before and after: iam:CreatePolicyVersion went from allowed to denied by the boundary, without the widening ever being attached.

Decommissioned

Both IAM resources were deleted at the end — the boundary removed first, then the policy and the role — and two NoSuchEntity reads confirmed they were gone. Nothing was ever attached to a principal that could act. The policy files and the full simulator output in the repo reproduce every result; IAM and the simulator cost nothing.

What it does

Summary

Identity policies grow one reviewed change at a time, and one added statement — iam:CreatePolicyVersion — is enough to let a role rewrite the policy meant to restrict it. A permissions boundary sets a ceiling regardless of what gets attached later. I created an inert role, wrote widened.json as a proposed identity policy, and simulated it against the role with no boundary: CreatePolicyVersion against BoundaryProofCeiling came back allowed. Then I set BoundaryProofCeiling as the role's boundary and ran the identical simulation: implicitDeny, with AllowedByPermissionsBoundary: false.

How it is wired

The mechanism

Why simulate instead of trying the actions? Firing real IAM mutations to see whether they're blocked changes the account you're trying to protect. simulate-principal-policy --policy-source-arn <role> --policy-input-list file://widened.json evaluates a proposed policy in the role's context, including its current boundary, and the widening never touches a live principal.

Why run the unbounded case at all? Without it, a later deny could just mean the action was never grantable, and the boundary would get credit it didn't earn. The role, policy, action and resource are identical in both runs; the boundary is the only thing that changes.

Why read AllowedByPermissionsBoundary, not just the decision? With the boundary attached, CreatePolicyVersion and ListUsers both return implicitDeny. Only the boundary flag (false versus true) shows the ceiling caused one and a missing grant caused the other.

Why is the role inert? Its trust policy names ec2.amazonaws.com with no instance profile and it has no attached policies, so it exists as a policy source and boundary target but nothing can assume it and it holds no permissions.

What I verified, and how

Evidence, separated from design intent

Building something and being able to prove it works are different claims, so I've kept them separate below. Measured means I created the condition myself and recorded what happened. Observed means I looked at the simulator output and saw the state it was already in. By design means the configuration guarantees it, but I didn't run a test against it — better to say that plainly than dress it up as a result.

Measured

CreatePolicyVersion flipped from allowed to denied by the boundary

Against BoundaryProofCeiling as the resource, the unbounded run returned allowed. After put-role-permissions-boundary, the identical run returned implicitDeny with AllowedByPermissionsBoundary: false. Nothing else changed between the runs.

Method simulate-principal-policy before and after; full JSON output in results/.

Measured

The ceiling composes with Deny and grants nothing on its own

With the boundary attached, iam:GetRole (in both) stayed allowed; the five role mutations, including DeleteRolePermissionsBoundary, were explicitDeny with the boundary flag true; and iam:ListUsers, inside the ceiling but granted by nothing, was implicitDeny with the flag true.

Method Same bounded simulation, reading EvalDecision and PermissionsBoundaryDecisionDetail per action.

Measured

Eight predictions, eight matches

All eight decisions went into predictions.csv and were committed before the role or policy existed, then scored against the simulator: 8/8.

Method Compared the committed predictions with the simulator output.

Observed

Teardown left nothing behind

The boundary came off first — IAM won't delete a policy still in use as one — then the policy and role were deleted, and get-role and get-policy both returned NoSuchEntity. That output lives in a screenshot, not the repo.

Method Ran the two reads after deletion (screenshot below).

By design

The role couldn't have been used during the test

Its trust policy names EC2 with no instance profile, and it had no attached or inline policies. I didn't try to assume it.

Method Reviewed trust.json and the role's attachments.

Five claims. three measured, one observed, one stated as a design property rather than dressed up as a result.

What actually broke

Three failures worth keeping

--policy-input-list wants strings, not objects

Passing the widened policy as an ordinary JSON document failed. The CLI expects a list of policy strings, so widened.json became a one-element array holding the escaped document. That took longer than any other step.

The seal tag landed one commit early

At close-out, git tag -a v1.0.0 failed with tag 'v1.0.0' already exists: it had been created at the adjudication commit, before teardown. That contradicts the closing line of results/check-manifest.md. I left the tag where it landed and documented the discrepancy rather than moving it.

The teardown proof is a screenshot

Every other check pasted its full output into results/. The two NoSuchEntity reads were only captured on screen, which makes them the weakest evidence in the project.
